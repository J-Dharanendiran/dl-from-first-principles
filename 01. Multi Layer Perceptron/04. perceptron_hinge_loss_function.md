## Right or Wrong Isn't the Whole Story

By now the Perceptron has a simple way of learning: look at a point, ask *"did I classify this correctly?"*, and if not, nudge the line.

That question has exactly two possible answers, **yes** or **no**. And that's both the algorithm's charm and its blind spot.

Consider two correctly classified positive points. One sits right next to the decision boundary, a hair's breadth from being wrong. The other sits far inside the positive region, safe and comfortable. The Perceptron's view of them: *"Both correct. Nothing to see here."*

Is that really the right view? Let's put numbers on it.

> ⚠️ **A quick notation change.** In earlier chapters the labels were $0$ and $1$. In this chapter they are $-1$ and $+1$. This makes the loss formulas much shorter, because the sign of $y$ can multiply the score directly. Nothing else about the model changes: the score is still $f(x) = w_1x_1 + w_2x_2 + b$, and the predicted class is still the sign of $f(x)$.

---

## Perceptron Loss, Precisely

For a point with true label $y_i \in \{-1, +1\}$ and score $f(x_i)$, the **Perceptron loss** is:

$$\boxed{L_i = \max\big(0,\; -y_i f(x_i)\big)}$$

The key quantity is the product $y_i f(x_i)$:

- **Positive** → the score and the label agree in sign → the point is **correct**.
- **Negative** → they disagree → the point is **wrong**.

Why does the formula work? Work through the two cases.

**If the point is correct** ($y_if(x_i) > 0$): then $-y_if(x_i) < 0$, and $\max(0, \text{negative}) = 0$. No loss.

**If the point is wrong** ($y_if(x_i) < 0$): then $-y_if(x_i) > 0$, so the loss is that positive number, which is **how far on the wrong side** the point is.

So Perceptron loss is zero for every correct point and grows the deeper a point sits in the wrong region.

---

## The Blind Spot: Everything Correct Looks the Same

Take two positive points ($y = +1$):

| Point | Score $f(x)$ | $y\,f(x)$ | Perceptron loss |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | $2$ | $2$ | $\max(0, -2) = 0$ |
| B | $10$ | $10$ | $\max(0, -10) = 0$ |

Both correct, both zero loss. But A is close to the boundary and B is far from it. **Perceptron loss cannot tell them apart.** Once a point is on the right side, the loss stops caring how far inside it is.

*Why might that matter?* Because a boundary that squeezes right up against correctly classified points is fragile. A tiny shift in a new data point can push it to the wrong side. A boundary that leaves breathing room on both sides is more trustworthy.

---

## Hinge Loss: Adding a Margin

The fix is small and elegant. Instead of demanding $y f(x) > 0$ (merely correct), demand $y f(x) \ge 1$ (**correct with room to spare**):

$$\boxed{L_i = \max\big(0,\; 1 - y_i f(x_i)\big)}$$

That "1" is a **margin**. Compare the two losses on the same points:

| $y\,f(x)$ | Situation | Perceptron loss | Hinge loss |
|---:|---|---:|---:|
| $-1$ | Wrong | $1$ | $2$ |
| $0.5$ | Correct, but too close | $0$ | $0.5$ |
| $1$ | Correct, exactly at the margin | $0$ | $0$ |
| $5$ | Correct, comfortably | $0$ | $0$ |

Check one by hand: for $y f(x) = 0.5$, hinge loss is $\max(0, 1 - 0.5) = 0.5$. ✅

So the two losses ask different questions:

- **Perceptron loss:** *Is the point correctly classified?*
- **Hinge loss:** *Is the point correctly classified **with a sufficient margin**?*

This difference is the conceptual bridge from the Perceptron to **Support Vector Machines**.

## "Where does this actually happen in an example?"

It's a fair challenge. A claim about two loss functions means little until you see it happen on real points with real parameters. So let's build an example in which every number comes from the parameters, with nothing pulled out of thin air.

> 💡 **Rule for honest examples:** if a score appears in a table, you should be able to point to the weights and bias that produced it. A convenient number with no derivation isn't a demonstration.

Start with two labelled points:

| Point | Coordinates | Label |
|---|---|---|
| A | $(2, 3)$ | $+1$ |
| B | $(4, 1)$ | $-1$ |

and the model $w_1 = 1,\; w_2 = 1,\; b = -4$, so:

$$f(x) = x_1 + x_2 - 4$$

**Scores:**

$$f(A) = 2 + 3 - 4 = 1 \qquad f(B) = 4 + 1 - 4 = 1$$

**Point A** has label $+1$ and score $+1$: correct ($y f = 1$). **Point B** has label $-1$ but score $+1$: **wrong** ($y f = -1$).

**Perceptron loss:**

$$L_A = \max(0, -1) = 0 \qquad L_B = \max(0, -(-1)(1)) = \max(0, 1) = 1$$

$$\text{Average} = \frac{0 + 1}{2} = \boxed{0.5}$$

---

## A Tie That Proves Nothing

Now change the bias to $b = -6$:

$$f(A) = 2 + 3 - 6 = -1 \;\Rightarrow\; y f = -1 \;\text{(wrong)}$$

$$f(B) = 4 + 1 - 6 = -1 \;\Rightarrow\; y f = (-1)(-1) = +1 \;\text{(correct)}$$

$$L_A = 1, \quad L_B = 0, \quad \text{Average} = \boxed{0.5}$$

Same average Perceptron loss as before (0.5), but **different points are wrong**. The loss can't tell these two situations apart.

That looks like a limitation, but be careful about what it actually shows. Let's compute the **hinge** loss on both configurations:

| Configuration | $y_Af(A)$ | $y_Bf(B)$ | Hinge $L_A$ | Hinge $L_B$ | Average hinge |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| $b = -4$ | $1$ | $-1$ | $0$ | $2$ | $1.0$ |
| $b = -6$ | $-1$ | $1$ | $2$ | $0$ | $1.0$ |

**Hinge loss ties as well.** The reason is the data, not the loss: both points have $x_1 + x_2 = 5$, so $f(A) = f(B) = 5 + b$ always. Moving $b$ just mirrors the situation between the two points. This pair is too symmetric to show anything about margins.

> 🔍 **Lesson:** this tie shows that *an average loss alone doesn't say which points are wrong*. It does **not** show a difference between Perceptron and hinge loss. Don't use it as evidence for the margin story.

---

## An Example That Does Show the Margin

To see the margin effect, we need a case where **both loss functions agree the classification is perfect**, yet the boundaries are clearly different in quality.

Use new points with a fixed $w_1 = w_2 = 1$ (so $f(x) = x_1 + x_2 + b$):

| Point | Coordinates | Label | $x_1 + x_2$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | $(2, 3)$ | $+1$ | $5$ |
| B | $(1, 1)$ | $-1$ | $2$ |

Compare two choices of bias.

### Boundary 1: $b = -3.5$ (centred between the points)

$$f(A) = 5 - 3.5 = 1.5 \;\Rightarrow\; y_Af(A) = 1.5$$

$$f(B) = 2 - 3.5 = -1.5 \;\Rightarrow\; y_Bf(B) = (-1)(-1.5) = 1.5$$

Both correct. Perceptron loss: $0$ and $0$. Hinge loss: $\max(0, 1-1.5) = 0$ and $0$.

### Boundary 2: $b = -2.2$ (hugging point B)

$$f(A) = 5 - 2.2 = 2.8 \;\Rightarrow\; y_Af(A) = 2.8$$

$$f(B) = 2 - 2.2 = -0.2 \;\Rightarrow\; y_Bf(B) = (-1)(-0.2) = 0.2$$

Both still correct. **Perceptron loss: $0$ and $0$.** Hinge loss:

$$L_A = \max(0, 1 - 2.8) = 0 \qquad L_B = \max(0, 1 - 0.2) = 0.8$$

$$\text{Average hinge} = \frac{0 + 0.8}{2} = 0.4$$

### Side by side

| | Perceptron loss | Average hinge loss |
|---|---:|---:|
| Boundary 1 ($b = -3.5$) | $0$ | $0$ |
| Boundary 2 ($b = -2.2$) | $0$ | $0.4$ |

**There it is.** The Perceptron is equally satisfied with both boundaries. Hinge loss prefers Boundary 1, because Boundary 2 leaves point B only $0.2$ score-units from the boundary.

How big is that range of "perfectly fine" boundaries? Any $b$ that classifies both points correctly works for Perceptron loss: $-5 < b < -2$. Hinge loss is zero only for $-4 \le b \le -3$, a much narrower band **centred** between the points. (Both ranges were checked numerically.) Hinge loss gently pushes the boundary toward the middle of the gap.

---

## What Each Loss Does to the Learning Rule

Recall the Perceptron learning rule: update only when a point is wrong. Now we can see *why* it looks the way it does. Take the gradient of each loss with respect to the weights.

**Perceptron loss** $\max(0, -yf(x))$ has gradient:

$$\nabla_w L = \begin{cases} -y\,x & \text{if } y f(x) < 0 \;\text{(wrong)} \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

A gradient-descent step $w \leftarrow w - \eta\nabla_wL$ gives:

$$w \leftarrow w + \eta\, y\, x \quad\text{(only on mistakes)}$$

This is the same rule from the training chapter in new notation. With labels $\{-1,+1\}$: a missed positive adds $\eta x$, a missed negative subtracts $\eta x$. ✅ **The Perceptron learning rule is gradient descent on Perceptron loss.**

**Hinge loss** $\max(0, 1 - yf(x))$ has gradient:

$$\nabla_w L = \begin{cases} -y\,x & \text{if } y f(x) < 1 \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

Same update, **but the trigger changed**: from "$yf(x) < 0$" (wrong) to "$yf(x) < 1$" (wrong **or** correct-but-too-close). So under hinge loss, a correctly classified point sitting inside the margin still pushes the boundary away from itself, while points outside the margin leave it alone.

| | Updates when... |
|---|---|
| Perceptron loss | $y\,f(x) < 0$ (misclassified) |
| Hinge loss | $y\,f(x) < 1$ (misclassified *or* inside the margin) |

---

## A Catch: Margin of 1 Can Be Cheated

There's a loophole, and it's worth seeing now because it explains how SVMs are actually built.

"Margin of 1" is measured in **score units**, and scores scale with the weights. Take Boundary 2 and multiply every parameter by 10: $w_1 = w_2 = 10$, $b = -22$.

This is the **same geometric line** ($10(x_1 + x_2) = 22 \Leftrightarrow x_1 + x_2 = 2.2$). But now:

$$f(A) = 10(5) - 22 = 28 \qquad f(B) = 10(2) - 22 = -2 \;\Rightarrow\; y_Bf(B) = 2$$

$$L_B = \max(0, 1 - 2) = 0$$

Hinge loss is now **zero**, though the boundary hasn't moved at all and still hugs point B. (Verified in code; the geometric distance from B to the line is still about $0.14$ in both cases.)

Real distance from a point to the boundary is $\dfrac{|f(x)|}{\lVert w\rVert}$, so a "score margin of 1" means a real gap of $\dfrac{1}{\lVert w\rVert}$. A big $\lVert w\rVert$ means a tiny real margin.

The fix is to **penalise large weights** alongside the hinge loss. That extra term is the missing piece that, together with hinge loss, defines the Support Vector Machine. It forces the model to achieve the margin of 1 using the *smallest possible* weights, which means the *widest possible real gap*.

## Sanity Check

- [ ] This chapter uses labels $\{-1, +1\}$; earlier chapters used $\{0, 1\}$.
- [ ] $y f(x) > 0$ means correct; the magnitude of $yf(x)$ says how confidently.
- [ ] Perceptron loss: $\max(0, -yf)$. Zero for *every* correct point, however close to the boundary.
- [ ] Hinge loss: $\max(0, 1 - yf)$. Zero only when $yf \ge 1$.
- [ ] A tie in *average* loss between two parameter sets doesn't mean the two sets make the same mistakes.
- [ ] The symmetric pair $A(2,3), B(4,1)$ ties under **both** losses, so it's a poor example of the margin effect.
- [ ] The pair $A(2,3), B(1,1)$ shows it: Perceptron loss $0$ for both boundaries; hinge $0$ vs $0.4$.
- [ ] Perceptron rule $=$ gradient descent on Perceptron loss. Hinge loss updates when $yf < 1$.
- [ ] Hinge loss alone can be satisfied by scaling the weights up; real margins need a penalty on $\lVert w\rVert$.
- [ ] Every number in an example should be traceable to weights and bias.

## ML Bridge

- **Support Vector Machines:** hinge loss plus a penalty on $\lVert w\rVert^2$. The first term demands a margin; the second makes the margin *real* by keeping weights small.
- **Choosing a loss = choosing what to care about:** Perceptron loss cares about correctness; hinge loss adds confidence; log loss (Logistic Regression) goes further and cares about calibrated probabilities. Same score $f(x)$, different questions asked of it.
- **Subgradients:** both losses have a kink where the $\max$ switches, so "gradient" really means a subgradient. In practice, plain gradient-descent-style updates still work.
- **Margins beyond SVMs:** the idea that "confidently correct is better than barely correct" shows up in many modern training objectives.

---

## Deepest Realization

🌀 **A loss function is not a measurement of the model. It's a statement of what you care about.**

Perceptron loss says: *"I only care that you're right."* Hinge loss says: *"I care that you're right **and** not close to wrong."* Neither is more true than the other. They encode different values, and the model, being obedient, becomes exactly what the loss rewards.

## Everything in One Table

| Concept | In one line |
|---|---|
| Labels here | $y \in \{-1, +1\}$, so $yf(x) > 0$ means correct |
| Perceptron loss | $\max(0, -yf(x))$: zero for all correct points |
| Blind spot | Can't distinguish barely-correct from confidently-correct |
| Hinge loss | $\max(0, 1 - yf(x))$: zero only when correct with margin $\ge 1$ |
| Margin | The "1": required confidence beyond merely being correct |
| Symmetric example $(2,3),(4,1)$ | Ties under both losses; not a valid margin demo |
| Margin example $(2,3),(1,1)$ | Perceptron: $0$ vs $0$; hinge: $0$ vs $0.4$ |
| Perceptron update | $w \leftarrow w + \eta yx$ when $yf < 0$ |
| Hinge update | $w \leftarrow w + \eta yx$ when $yf < 1$ |
| Catch | Scaling $w$ up fakes a margin; SVMs penalise $\lVert w\rVert$ |
| Real distance to boundary | $\lvert f(x)\rvert / \lVert w\rVert$ |

## Worked Example: Training a Perceptron by Hand

Time to run the whole machine once, start to finish, on a tiny dataset. The rules for this section:

- **Every formula is written out before it is used.** Nothing is "derived" on the fly; each formula comes from earlier chapters and is only *applied* here.
- **Every arithmetic step is shown**, with the numbers substituted in.
- **Every claim is checked** by recomputing the score after each update.

### The setup

Four students, two features each, labels $-1$ / $+1$:

| Point | $(x_1, x_2)$ | Label $y$ |
|---|---|---:|
| P1 | $(3, 3)$ | $+1$ |
| P2 | $(1, 1)$ | $-1$ |
| P3 | $(4, 2)$ | $+1$ |
| P4 | $(2, 0)$ | $-1$ |

Choices made up front, so nothing is hidden:

- **Starting weights:** $W = [w_0, w_1, w_2] = [-3, -1, -1]$, picked by hand.
- **Learning rate:** $\eta = 1$, which keeps the arithmetic clean.
- **Order:** P1 → P2 → P3 → P4, then repeat. (Earlier chapters picked points at random; a fixed order makes the trace reproducible.)
- **Tie rule:** a score of exactly $0$ is predicted as $+1$. (No point in this example lands on a tie while training.)

### The five formulas used

| # | Name | Formula |
|---|---|---|
| F1 | Augmented input | $X = [1,\, x_1,\, x_2]$ |
| F2 | Score | $z = W\cdot X = w_0\cdot 1 + w_1x_1 + w_2x_2$ |
| F3 | Prediction | $\hat y = +1$ if $z \ge 0$, otherwise $\hat y = -1$ |
| F4 | Perceptron loss | $L = \max(0,\,-y\cdot z)$ |
| F5 | Update (only when $\hat y \ne y$) | $W_{\text{new}} = W_{\text{old}} + \eta\,y\,X$ |

F5 is the learning rule from the training chapter in $\pm1$ notation: a missed positive adds $\eta X$, a missed negative subtracts $\eta X$.

### Round 1

#### Step 1: visit P1, $(3, 3)$, $y = +1$

Current $W = [-3, -1, -1]$.

- **F1:** $X = [1, 3, 3]$
- **F2:** $z = (-3)(1) + (-1)(3) + (-1)(3) = -3 - 3 - 3 = -9$
- **F3:** $z = -9 < 0$, so $\hat y = -1$
- **Compare:** $\hat y = -1$ but $y = +1$, so this is a **mistake** (a missed positive).
- **F4:** $y\cdot z = (+1)(-9) = -9$, so $L = \max(0, 9) = 9$
- **F5:** $W_{\text{new}} = [-3, -1, -1] + (1)(+1)[1, 3, 3]$

$$W_{\text{new}} = [-3 + 1,\; -1 + 3,\; -1 + 3] = [-2,\; 2,\; 2]$$

**Check:** score of P1 under the new weights:

$$z = (-2)(1) + (2)(3) + (2)(3) = -2 + 6 + 6 = 10$$

It rose from $-9$ to $10$, an increase of $19$. That matches $\eta\,\lVert X\rVert^2 = 1\cdot(1^2 + 3^2 + 3^2) = 1 + 9 + 9 = 19$. ✅ (P1 is now correct. Larger steps can overshoot like this, which is why $\eta$ matters.)

#### Step 2: visit P2, $(1, 1)$, $y = -1$

Current $W = [-2, 2, 2]$.

- **F1:** $X = [1, 1, 1]$
- **F2:** $z = (-2)(1) + (2)(1) + (2)(1) = -2 + 2 + 2 = 2$
- **F3:** $z = 2 \ge 0$, so $\hat y = +1$
- **Compare:** $\hat y = +1$ but $y = -1$, so this is a **mistake** (a missed negative).
- **F4:** $y\cdot z = (-1)(2) = -2$, so $L = \max(0, 2) = 2$
- **F5:** $W_{\text{new}} = [-2, 2, 2] + (1)(-1)[1, 1, 1]$

$$W_{\text{new}} = [-2 - 1,\; 2 - 1,\; 2 - 1] = [-3,\; 1,\; 1]$$

**Check:** score of P2 under the new weights:

$$z = (-3)(1) + (1)(1) + (1)(1) = -3 + 1 + 1 = -1$$

It fell from $2$ to $-1$, a drop of $3$. That matches $\eta\,\lVert X\rVert^2 = 1^2 + 1^2 + 1^2 = 3$. ✅ (P2 is now correct.)

#### Step 3: visit P3, $(4, 2)$, $y = +1$

Current $W = [-3, 1, 1]$.

- **F1:** $X = [1, 4, 2]$
- **F2:** $z = (-3)(1) + (1)(4) + (1)(2) = -3 + 4 + 2 = 3$
- **F3:** $z = 3 \ge 0$, so $\hat y = +1$
- **Compare:** $\hat y = y$, so **correct**.
- **F4:** $y\cdot z = (+1)(3) = 3$, so $L = \max(0, -3) = 0$
- **F5:** not applied. $W$ stays $[-3, 1, 1]$.

#### Step 4: visit P4, $(2, 0)$, $y = -1$

Current $W = [-3, 1, 1]$.

- **F1:** $X = [1, 2, 0]$
- **F2:** $z = (-3)(1) + (1)(2) + (1)(0) = -3 + 2 + 0 = -1$
- **F3:** $z = -1 < 0$, so $\hat y = -1$
- **Compare:** $\hat y = y$, so **correct**.
- **F4:** $y\cdot z = (-1)(-1) = 1$, so $L = \max(0, -1) = 0$
- **F5:** not applied. $W$ stays $[-3, 1, 1]$.

### Round 1 summary

| Step | Point | $z$ | $\hat y$ | $y$ | Result | $L$ | $W$ after |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---|---:|---|
| 1 | P1 | $-9$ | $-1$ | $+1$ | ❌ update | $9$ | $[-2, 2, 2]$ |
| 2 | P2 | $2$ | $+1$ | $-1$ | ❌ update | $2$ | $[-3, 1, 1]$ |
| 3 | P3 | $3$ | $+1$ | $+1$ | ✅ | $0$ | $[-3, 1, 1]$ |
| 4 | P4 | $-1$ | $-1$ | $-1$ | ✅ | $0$ | $[-3, 1, 1]$ |

Two mistakes, two updates. (The losses here are measured *at the moment each point was visited*, while $W$ was still changing, so they are a running record and not the loss of one fixed model.)

### Round 2: does anything still go wrong?

Convergence means a full pass with **zero** mistakes. Re-score all four points with the fixed $W = [-3, 1, 1]$:

| Point | $X$ | $z = -3 + x_1 + x_2$ | $\hat y$ | $y$ | Correct? |
|---|---|---|---:|---:|---|
| P1 | $[1, 3, 3]$ | $-3 + 3 + 3 = 3$ | $+1$ | $+1$ | ✅ |
| P2 | $[1, 1, 1]$ | $-3 + 1 + 1 = -1$ | $-1$ | $-1$ | ✅ |
| P3 | $[1, 4, 2]$ | $-3 + 4 + 2 = 3$ | $+1$ | $+1$ | ✅ |
| P4 | $[1, 2, 0]$ | $-3 + 2 + 0 = -1$ | $-1$ | $-1$ | ✅ |

Zero mistakes, so no update fires and the weights never change again. **Training has converged.**

### The weights across training

| After | $w_0$ | $w_1$ | $w_2$ |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Start | $-3$ | $-1$ | $-1$ |
| Update 1 (P1) | $-2$ | $2$ | $2$ |
| Update 2 (P2) | $-3$ | $1$ | $1$ |

### Reading the learned line

Final weights: $w_0 = -3$, $w_1 = 1$, $w_2 = 1$. The boundary is $w_0 + w_1x_1 + w_2x_2 = 0$. Substitute:

$$-3 + 1\cdot x_1 + 1\cdot x_2 = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x_1 + x_2 = 3$$

Now convert to slope and intercept with the formulas from the training chapter, $m = -\frac{w_1}{w_2}$ and $q = -\frac{w_0}{w_2}$:

$$m = -\frac{1}{1} = -1 \qquad\qquad q = -\frac{-3}{1} = 3$$

So the line is $x_2 = -x_1 + 3$.

**Check against the data:** P1 has $x_1 + x_2 = 3 + 3 = 6 > 3$, so it is on the positive side. P2 has $1 + 1 = 2 < 3$, so it is on the negative side. ✅

### Predicting new students

Prediction reuses F1, F2 and F3 with the frozen weights:

| New point | $X$ | $z = -3 + x_1 + x_2$ | $\hat y$ |
|---|---|---|---:|
| $(4, 3)$ | $[1, 4, 3]$ | $-3 + 4 + 3 = 4$ | $+1$ |
| $(1, 0)$ | $[1, 1, 0]$ | $-3 + 1 + 0 = -2$ | $-1$ |
| $(2, 1)$ | $[1, 2, 1]$ | $-3 + 2 + 1 = 0$ | $+1$ (the tie rule: exactly on the line) |

### Loss check on the final model

With $W = [-3, 1, 1]$, the products $y\cdot z$ for P1 to P4 are $3,\; 1,\; 3,\; 1$.

| Loss | Formula | P1 | P2 | P3 | P4 | Total |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Perceptron | $\max(0, -yz)$ | $0$ | $0$ | $0$ | $0$ | $0$ |
| Hinge | $\max(0, 1 - yz)$ | $\max(0, -2) = 0$ | $\max(0, 0) = 0$ | $\max(0, -2) = 0$ | $\max(0, 0) = 0$ | $0$ |

Both losses are zero. P2 and P4 have $y\cdot z = 1$, so they sit **exactly on the edge of the margin**. That is a coincidence of the integer weights here, and the margin warning from earlier still applies: a different valid boundary can score worse on hinge loss.

### One more honest observation

This line, $x_1 + x_2 = 3$, is not special. The negatives have $x_1 + x_2 = 2$ and the positives have $x_1 + x_2 = 6$, so *any* threshold strictly between 2 and 6 separates the data. The Perceptron stopped at 3 because of where it started and the order it visited points. I reran the same algorithm from $W = [-4, -1, -1]$ and it stopped at $x_1 + x_2 = 4$ instead. Both are valid; neither is "best".

### Verify it yourself

The same trace in code (I ran this; it reproduces Round 1 and Round 2 exactly):

```python
import numpy as np

pts = [((3, 3), +1), ((1, 1), -1), ((4, 2), +1), ((2, 0), -1)]
W = np.array([-3.0, -1.0, -1.0])   # [w0, w1, w2]
eta = 1.0

for rnd in range(1, 4):
    mistakes = 0
    for (x1, x2), y in pts:
        X = np.array([1.0, x1, x2])        # F1
        z = W @ X                          # F2
        y_hat = 1 if z >= 0 else -1        # F3
        if y_hat != y:
            W = W + eta * y * X            # F5
            mistakes += 1
    print(f"round {rnd}: W={W}, mistakes={mistakes}")
    if mistakes == 0:
        break
```

Output:

```
round 1: W=[-3.  1.  1.], mistakes=2
round 2: W=[-3.  1.  1.], mistakes=0
```

### ✅ Final Takeaway

> **"Perceptron loss asks whether a point is correct; hinge loss asks whether it is correct with room to spare. That one extra unit of margin is the seed from which Support Vector Machines grow."**
