## The Question We Left Open

You already know how a Perceptron **predicts**. Give it inputs, multiply by weights, add the bias, run the result through a step function, and out comes a 0 or a 1.

But one question was left hanging, and it's the whole point of this chapter:

> **"We know how a perceptron makes predictions, but how does it learn the correct weights and bias?"** 🤔

Make it concrete. Each student has two features:

- $X_1$ = CGPA
- $X_2$ = IQ

and one outcome $Y$: placed (1) or not placed (0). We want a Perceptron that predicts placement. But at the start, **nobody tells us which weights and bias will classify the students correctly.** We have to find them.

One note on notation before we go further. Earlier, the bias was written $b$. From here on it's written $w_0$, the weight of an always-on input of 1. They're the same thing:

$$w_0 + w_1x_1 + w_2x_2 \quad\equiv\quad b + w_1x_1 + w_2x_2$$

## The Core Idea: Fix the Line Using Its Mistakes

Here's a picture to hold on to. 🎯

Plot the students: 🟢 green for placed, 🔵 blue for not placed. If a single straight line can separate the two colours, the data is **linearly separable**. (With two features the boundary is a line; with more, a hyperplane.)

Now suppose we draw a line at random. It's bad: some placed students land on the "not placed" side, and some unplaced students land on the "placed" side.

*"I can't see the perfect line directly. So how do I get there?"*

You don't need to. The trick is to **never search for the perfect line**. Instead, repeat this loop:

1. Pick a random student.
2. Check: does the current line classify them correctly?
3. **If yes**, leave the line alone.
4. **If no**, move the line a little toward fixing that student.
5. Repeat.

Over many rounds, the line wobbles into a position that classifies the training points correctly. This is the **Perceptron Trick**:

> *Don't find the right line. Take a wrong line and let its mistakes fix it.*

To make this work, three questions need answers:

- How do I tell which side of a line is "positive"?
- How can I actually *move* a line?
- How far should I move it?

Let's take them one by one.

---

## Which Side Is Positive?

*"How do I even know which side of a line is the positive side?"*

You might be tempted to say "above the line" or "to the right." That's unreliable, since lines can tilt any way. The dependable method is to **plug the point into the equation**.

Take the boundary:

$$2x + 3y + 5 = 0$$

| Condition | Region |
|---|---|
| $2x + 3y + 5 > 0$ | Positive |
| $2x + 3y + 5 < 0$ | Negative |
| $2x + 3y + 5 = 0$ | On the boundary |

Try two points.

**Point $(1, 1)$:**

$$2(1) + 3(1) + 5 = 2 + 3 + 5 = 10 > 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \text{positive}$$

**Point $(-5, 0)$:**

$$2(-5) + 3(0) + 5 = -10 + 5 = -5 < 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \text{negative}$$

> 💡 **Rule:** you never judge the side by eye. You *evaluate the equation* at the point and read the sign.

---

## Three Dials That Move a Line

The general line is $ax + by + c = 0$. It has exactly three numbers, and each changes the line differently. These are the dials we'll turn during training.

### Dial 1: the constant $c$ slides the line

Start with $2x + 3y + 5 = 0$, and change the constant from 5 to 0:

$$2x + 3y + 0 = 0$$

The line's tilt is untouched; it only **shifts**. This is a **parallel translation**.

Why? Solve for $y$:

$$y = -\frac{a}{b}x - \frac{c}{b}$$

The slope $-\frac{a}{b}$ doesn't contain $c$. Only the intercept $-\frac{c}{b}$ does. In our example, going from $c = 5$ to $c = 0$ moves the intercept from $-\frac{5}{3}$ up to $0$, so the line moves **up**. (Which way a line moves depends on the sign of $b$; the lasting idea is that $c$ shifts the line without turning it.)

### Dial 2: the coefficient of $x$ tilts the line

Change $2x + 3y + 5 = 0$ to $5x + 3y + 5 = 0$. The slope goes from $-\frac{2}{3}$ to $-\frac{5}{3}$, so the line **rotates**.

### Dial 3: the coefficient of $y$ tilts it too

Change it to $2x + 5y + 5 = 0$. The slope becomes $-\frac{2}{5}$, and again the line **rotates**.

| Change this | Effect on the line |
|---|---|
| $c$ (the bias) | Slides it, parallel translation |
| $a$ (coefficient of $x$) | Changes tilt |
| $b$ (coefficient of $y$) | Changes tilt |

In Perceptron language, $c \leftrightarrow w_0$, $a \leftrightarrow w_1$, $b \leftrightarrow w_2$. Training a Perceptron means **turning these three dials, a little at a time, until the line separates the classes**.

---

## Moving the Line Using a Misclassified Point

Say the current line is $2x + 3y + 5 = 0$ and we've found a student it gets wrong. We want to turn the three dials so the line moves in the right direction.

*"Do I just adjust the numbers randomly and hope?"*

No. The dials are adjusted using **the misclassified point's own coordinates**. The mistake itself tells us which way to move.

### A neat bookkeeping trick: append a 1

Our boundary has three coefficients, but a student has only two features. Let's make the shapes match by writing the student $(4, 5)$ not as $[4, 5]$ but as:

$$X = [1,\; 4,\; 5]$$

Why the extra 1? Because the boundary is $w_0 + w_1x_1 + w_2x_2$, and writing the weights and the augmented input as vectors gives:

$$W = [w_0, w_1, w_2], \qquad X = [1, x_1, x_2]$$

$$W \cdot X = w_0\cdot 1 + w_1x_1 + w_2x_2$$

The leading 1 multiplies $w_0$, so the bias is absorbed into a single dot product. Now **all three dials can be updated in one operation**: $W$ changes by something proportional to $X$.

---

## Why Small Steps: The Learning Rate

*"We found a mistake. Should we jump the line all the way to fix it?"*

No. Imagine a tug-of-war where every misclassified point yanks the line hard. One unlucky point could fling the line far away and *ruin* what earlier points had fixed. So each update is shrunk by a number called the **learning rate**, written $\eta$:

$$\text{Change} = \eta \times X$$

- 🐘 **Large $\eta$:** big jumps, fast but jumpy.
- 🐢 **Small $\eta$:** tiny nudges, steady but slow.

---

## Two Mistakes, Two Updates

The Perceptron predicts with the step rule:

$$\hat y = \begin{cases} 1 & z \ge 0 \\ 0 & z < 0 \end{cases}, \qquad z = W\cdot X$$

Compare prediction with reality and you get four situations:

| Actual $y$ | Predicted $\hat y$ | Result |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1 | ✅ Correct |
| 0 | 0 | ✅ Correct |
| 1 | 0 | ❌ Wrong |
| 0 | 1 | ❌ Wrong |

Only the two ❌ rows need action.

### Mistake 1: actual 0, predicted 1

A student who was **not** placed sits on the positive side. Their score $z$ is too high, so we want to **lower** it:

$$\boxed{W_{\text{new}} = W_{\text{old}} - \eta X}$$

### Mistake 2: actual 1, predicted 0

A student who **was** placed sits on the negative side. Their score is too low, so we want to **raise** it:

$$\boxed{W_{\text{new}} = W_{\text{old}} + \eta X}$$

Subtract for a false positive, add for a false negative.

---

## Two Updates Become One Equation

Two cases, two formulas. Can we merge them? 🧩

Define the **error**:

$$e = y - \hat y$$

and tabulate it:

| Actual $y$ | Predicted $\hat y$ | Error $e = y - \hat y$ | What we want |
|---:|---:|---:|---|
| 1 | 1 | $0$ | No change |
| 0 | 0 | $0$ | No change |
| 1 | 0 | $+1$ | Add $\eta X$ |
| 0 | 1 | $-1$ | Subtract $\eta X$ |

The error is exactly the "add or subtract" signal: $0$ means do nothing, $+1$ means add, $-1$ means subtract. So one formula covers every case:

$$\boxed{W_{\text{new}} = W_{\text{old}} + \eta\,(y - \hat y)\,X}$$

Check it case by case:

- **Correct prediction:** $y - \hat y = 0$, so $W_{\text{new}} = W_{\text{old}}$. Nothing moves.
- **Positive point missed:** $y - \hat y = 1$, so $W_{\text{new}} = W_{\text{old}} + \eta X$.
- **Negative point missed:** $y - \hat y = -1$, so $W_{\text{new}} = W_{\text{old}} - \eta X$.

> 🔖 This is the **Perceptron learning rule**. If you remember one thing, remember this.

---

## Does One Update Really Help? A Numeric Check

It's fair to be suspicious. *"Adding $\eta X$ to the weights: does that genuinely push the point toward being classified correctly?"*

Let's prove it, then test it with numbers.

### The proof, step by step

After an update, the new score on the same point is:

$$z_{\text{new}} = W_{\text{new}}\cdot X = (W_{\text{old}} + \eta X)\cdot X$$

Distribute the dot product:

$$z_{\text{new}} = W_{\text{old}}\cdot X + \eta\,(X \cdot X)$$

$$z_{\text{new}} = z_{\text{old}} + \eta\,\lVert X\rVert^2$$

Since $X = [1, x_1, x_2]$, we get $\lVert X\rVert^2 = 1 + x_1^2 + x_2^2 > 0$, and $\eta > 0$. So adding $\eta X$ always **raises** the point's score by a positive amount. Likewise, subtracting always **lowers** it. The update pushes in exactly the right direction.

### Example A: a false positive

Current line: $W = [5, 2, 3]$ (that is, $2x + 3y + 5 = 0$). The point $(1, 1)$ has $X = [1, 1, 1]$ and score:

$$z = 5 + 2 + 3 = 10 \;\Rightarrow\; \hat y = 1$$

Suppose its true label is $y = 0$. The error is $y - \hat y = -1$. With $\eta = 0.1$:

$$W_{\text{new}} = [5,\,2,\,3] - 0.1\,[1,\,1,\,1] = [4.9,\; 1.9,\; 2.9]$$

New score on the same point:

$$z_{\text{new}} = 4.9 + 1.9 + 2.9 = 9.7$$

It dropped by $0.3$, and the formula agrees: $\eta\lVert X\rVert^2 = 0.1 \times 3 = 0.3$. ✅

**But the point is still classified positive.** One update didn't fix it. That's normal, and it's the whole reason for small steps. If only this point kept updating the line, each update lowers $z$ by $0.3$, so $z_k = 10 - 0.3k$. After 33 updates $z = 0.1$ (still $\ge 0$); after **34** updates $z = -0.2$ and the point finally flips. (I verified this by running the updates in code.) In real training, other points also pull on the line, so no single mistake gets fixed in isolation.

### Example B: a false negative

Same line $W = [5, 2, 3]$. The point $(-5, 0)$ has $X = [1, -5, 0]$ and score:

$$z = 5 - 10 + 0 = -5 \;\Rightarrow\; \hat y = 0$$

Suppose its true label is $y = 1$. The error is $+1$. With $\eta = 0.1$:

$$W_{\text{new}} = [5,\,2,\,3] + 0.1\,[1,\,-5,\,0] = [5.1,\; 1.5,\; 3.0]$$

New score:

$$z_{\text{new}} = 5.1 + 1.5(-5) + 3(0) = 5.1 - 7.5 = -2.4$$

It rose from $-5$ to $-2.4$ (an increase of $0.1 \times 26 = 2.6$ ✅), still negative but moving the right way.

Look at what happened to the line itself: $w_0$ (bias) went up, $w_1$ went down, and $w_2$ didn't move at all, because this point's second coordinate was 0. **Each weight moves in proportion to its own input**, so an input of 0 leaves its weight untouched.

## The Algorithm, Start to Finish

Now assemble everything:

1. **Start** with some initial weights $W = [w_0, w_1, w_2]$.
2. **Pick** a random training observation.
3. **Append 1:** $X = [1, x_1, x_2]$.
4. **Score it:** $z = W\cdot X$.
5. **Predict:** $\hat y = 1$ if $z \ge 0$, else $0$.
6. **Compute the error:** $e = y - \hat y$.
7. **Update:** $W \leftarrow W + \eta\,e\,X$.
8. **Repeat** from step 2.

A picture of one run, with a dataset of 100 students:

```
Round 1: pick student #42 → correct   → line stays
Round 2: pick student #7  → WRONG     → line shifts
Round 3: pick student #91 → correct   → line stays
Round 4: pick student #7  → still wrong → line shifts again
   ...
Eventually: every student classified correctly → line stops moving
```

When the line separates every training point, no more errors occur, every $e = 0$, and the weights stop changing. The line has **converged**.

## The Algorithm in Python

The steps translate almost one-to-one into code. Here is a complete function. I ran it on a linearly separable toy dataset, and it reached 100% training accuracy.

```python
import numpy as np

def perceptron(X, y, lr=0.1, n_iter=1000, seed=0):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)

    # append a column of 1s so the bias becomes w0
    X = np.insert(X, 0, 1, axis=1)

    # one weight per column: [w0, w1, w2]
    weights = np.ones(X.shape[1])

    for _ in range(n_iter):
        j = rng.integers(0, X.shape[0])        # pick a random student
        x_i = X[j]                             # [1, x1, x2]

        z = np.dot(x_i, weights)               # score
        y_pred = 1 if z >= 0 else 0            # step function

        weights = weights + lr * (y[j] - y_pred) * x_i   # the learning rule

    return weights[0], weights[1:]             # intercept, coefficients
```

Walk through the key lines:

| Line | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `np.insert(X, 0, 1, axis=1)` | Append 1 to every row (the bias trick) |
| `weights = np.ones(...)` | Starting point. Any starting values work, and `1`s are one common choice |
| `rng.integers(0, X.shape[0])` | Random student for this round |
| `np.dot(x_i, weights)` | $z = W \cdot X$ |
| `weights + lr * (y[j] - y_pred) * x_i` | $W + \eta\,(y-\hat y)\,X$ |

### One surprise worth noticing 🔍

When I ran this with three different random seeds on the same data, **all three runs separated the training data perfectly, yet they ended with three different lines** (different slopes and intercepts). That's not a bug.

If a dataset is linearly separable, infinitely many lines separate it. The Perceptron stops at the **first one it finds**, which depends on the starting weights and the random order of points. It gives you *a* valid line, not the *best* line. (Choosing the best line is a different problem for another day.)

## Drawing the Final Line

After training you have $[w_0, w_1, w_2]$ and want to plot the boundary. Plotting needs the familiar $y = mx + q$ form, so rearrange step by step.

Start from:

$$w_0 + w_1x_1 + w_2x_2 = 0$$

Move the terms with $x_2$ to one side:

$$w_2x_2 = -w_1x_1 - w_0$$

Divide by $w_2$ (assuming $w_2 \ne 0$):

$$x_2 = -\frac{w_1}{w_2}\,x_1 - \frac{w_0}{w_2}$$

Compare with $y = mx + q$:

$$\boxed{m = -\frac{w_1}{w_2}}\qquad\qquad \boxed{q = -\frac{w_0}{w_2}}$$

In the code above, `slope = -w[0]/w[1]` and `intercept = -b/w[1]` (there, `w` holds just the two feature weights and `b` is $w_0$).

If $w_2 = 0$ the formula breaks, because the line is **vertical** ($x_1 = -\frac{w_0}{w_1}$). The slope-intercept form simply can't describe a vertical line.

## When Does It Stop? The Assumption Hiding Underneath

Everything so far quietly assumed one thing: **the data is linearly separable**.

*"Will this loop always settle on a perfect line?"*

- **If the data is linearly separable:** yes. A standard result about this algorithm (the Perceptron convergence theorem) says it reaches a line that classifies every training point correctly after a finite number of updates.
- **If it isn't:** no. Some point is always misclassified, so updates never stop, and the line keeps bouncing around. No single straight line can get everything right.

There's also a practical wrinkle. The code above runs a fixed 1,000 rounds and doesn't *check* whether it has converged. "1,000 iterations" is a choice, not a guarantee. On a harder dataset it might need more rounds. And on non-separable data, it simply stops wherever the line happens to be after round 1,000.

## Sanity Check

- [ ] The sign of $ax + by + c$ at a point decides its side. Never judge by eye.
- [ ] $c$ slides the line; $a$ and $b$ tilt it (and, as a side effect, change where it crosses the axes).
- [ ] Appending a 1 to the input turns the bias into just another weight, $w_0$.
- [ ] Update only on mistakes. If $y = \hat y$ then $e = 0$ and nothing changes.
- [ ] $e = +1 \Rightarrow$ add $\eta X$; $e = -1 \Rightarrow$ subtract $\eta X$.
- [ ] One update raises (or lowers) the point's score by exactly $\eta\lVert X\rVert^2$ and does **not** guarantee the point flips.
- [ ] A weight whose input is 0 on that point doesn't move.
- [ ] Slope $m = -\frac{w_1}{w_2}$, intercept $q = -\frac{w_0}{w_2}$; this needs $w_2 \ne 0$.
- [ ] Convergence is guaranteed *only* for linearly separable data, and the result is *a* valid line, not *the* best one.
- [ ] $\eta$ is a step size you choose; it isn't learned.

## ML Bridge

This tiny loop contains ideas you'll meet everywhere:

- **Stochastic updates:** picking *one random sample at a time* and updating immediately is the same rhythm as **Stochastic Gradient Descent**. The Perceptron is an early, simple example of "learn from one example, nudge the weights."
- **The $(y - \hat y)$ pattern:** the error multiplied by the input shows up again in **Logistic Regression**, whose update is $W \leftarrow W + \eta\,\frac{1}{m}X^T(Y - \hat Y)$. The shape is the same; the difference is that $\hat y$ comes from a sigmoid (a smooth probability) instead of a hard step, and the update is derived from a loss function rather than from "move toward the mistake."
- **Learning rate:** the same $\eta$, with the same too-big or too-small trade-off, controls training in nearly every model you'll build.
- **Bias as a weight:** appending a column of 1s is exactly how Linear and Logistic Regression are written in matrix form.

---

## Deepest Realization

🌀 **The Perceptron never knows what a "good line" is. It only knows whether the current line got *this one point* wrong.**

There is no global picture and no plan. Just a repeated rule, *"if wrong, nudge toward the right answer,"* applied point after point. And that is enough to find a separating line, whenever one exists. Most of machine learning is a more sophisticated version of the same story: define what "wrong" means, and keep nudging.

### ✅ Final Takeaway

> **"A Perceptron learns by being wrong: every misclassified point nudges the weights toward its own correct answer, and when the data allows it, those small nudges are enough to settle on a line that separates the classes."**
